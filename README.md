# Chess with Decision Models: Jev vs Laya vs CLM

Can a model that **doesn't search and doesn't generate text** play chess? This project pits three "System One" decision models against each other. Each one looks at a position, scores the legal moves, and picks one in a single pass, with no engine and no lookahead at play time.

* **Jev** (TypeSafe): a hosted API, used zero-shot
* **Laya**: an open ~400M-parameter model, fine-tuned here on Stockfish-labelled positions
* **CLM-8B**: an open contrastive model (frozen Qwen3-8B + small heads), fine-tuned here the same way

## TL;DR

1. **Jev is by far the strongest zero-shot model.** It won 29 of 30 games against untrained opponents, all by checkmate.
2. **Fine-tuning lifts small open models to Jev's level.** Fine-tuned CLM scored 65% against Jev; fine-tuned Laya scored 40%.
3. **Training setup mattered more than model choice.** Given the same data, rule facts, and targets, a ~400M-parameter Laya matched the 8B-based CLM, in both per-move quality and head-to-head games.
4. **Untrained CLM is worse than random** at chess (17% against a random player).
5. **Converting a won position is the shared weakness.** Most games between the strong models end in draws.

Total API cost for the 50 tournament games involving Jev: **about $1.67**.

## The three models

These are decision models: instead of writing text, they answer typed questions (yes/no, multiple choice, scores) about a situation in one forward pass.

| | Jev | Laya | CLM-8B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Made by | [TypeSafe](https://typesafe.ai) | [Open source](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya) (Jev-compatible format) | [Contrastive-LM](https://huggingface.co/Contrastive-LM/CLM-v0.1-8B) (Stanford / NVIDIA researchers) |
| Access | Hosted API | Open weights, runs on a laptop | Open weights, needs a GPU (~16 GB) |
| Size | Not published | ~400M parameters (ModernBERT-large) | Frozen Qwen3-8B + two ~20M-parameter heads |
| How it was used | Zero-shot | Fully fine-tuned | Heads fine-tuned |
| Strength | Strong with no training | Cheap to run, fully trainable | Very cheap to fine-tune; scores every legal move at once |

## How a decision model plays chess

```
position + every legal move + rule facts  ──►  model  ──►  a score per move  ──►  play the best
```

* **No search at play time.** The code only supplies the rules of chess: the legal moves, what each move does ("captures a knight", "gives check"), and **rule facts** such as "this move repeats a position for the third time: draw", stalemate, the fifty-move rule, and the material balance.
* **Laya and Jev** answer one yes/no question per legal move ("Is this one of the best moves?") and play the highest-scoring move. **CLM** answers a single multiple-choice question with every legal move as an option.
* **Fine-tuning uses Stockfish as the teacher.** About 30,000 positions were generated by Stockfish self-play with added randomness, and Stockfish scored every legal move in each. The target is *relative*: how close each move is to the best move in that position.
* **The arena** plays deterministically. Each game starts with 4 random moves for variety, colours alternate, and games end by checkmate or the standard draw rules (threefold repetition and the fifty-move rule apply once they actually occur).

## Results

### Tournament (10 games per match)

Scores are from the **first-named** side's point of view (win = 1, draw = ½). "Unfinished" games reached the 300-half-move limit and are not counted in the score.

| Match | W | D | L | Unfinished | Score | Material | Jev cost | How games ended |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jev vs random | 9 | 1 | 0 | 0 | **95%** | +28.5 | $0.46 | checkmate 9, insufficient material 1 |
| Laya (base) vs random | 3 | 3 | 0 | 4 | 75% | +5.8 | – | move limit 4, checkmate 3, insufficient material 2, stalemate 1 |
| CLM (base) vs random | 0 | 2 | 4 | 4 | **17%** | −21.2 | – | checkmate 4, move limit 4, stalemate 1, repetition 1 |
| Jev vs Laya (base) | 10 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **100%** | +31.9 | $0.24 | checkmate 10 |
| Jev vs CLM (base) | 10 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **100%** | +30.3 | $0.24 | checkmate 10 |
| Laya (base) vs CLM (base) | 7 | 3 | 0 | 0 | 85% | +14.1 | – | checkmate 7, repetition 3 |
| Jev vs Laya v2 (fine-tuned) | 3 | 6 | 1 | 0 | 60% | +1.6 | $0.44 | insufficient material 5, checkmate 4, fifty-move rule 1 |
| Jev vs CLM (fine-tuned) | 1 | 5 | 4 | 0 | **35%** | −4.3 | $0.29 | checkmate 5, insufficient material 4, fifty-move rule 1 |
| Laya v2 vs CLM (fine-tuned) | 1 | 8 | 1 | 0 | 50% | −2.4 | – | repetition 7, checkmate 2, insufficient material 1 |

Longer matches (50 games each) point the same way: Laya v2 scored **53%** against fine-tuned CLM, **54%** against the first fine-tuned Laya, and **72%** (no losses) against base Laya.

### Move quality on 300 held-out positions

How much winning chance each model's chosen move gives up compared with Stockfish's best move (0 = perfect, lower is better). All models were scored on **the same 300 positions**.

| Model | Top-1 match* | Winning chance lost per move | Value lost (centipawn scale) | Reduction vs random |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random move | – | 0.231 | 0.337 | – |
| CLM (base, zero-shot) | 39.7% | 0.219 | 0.311 | 5% |
| Laya, first fine-tune | 53.3% | 0.146 | 0.215 | 37% |
| **Laya v2** (fine-tuned) | 57.7% | 0.103 | 0.159 | 55% |
| **CLM** (fine-tuned) | 58.3% | **0.098** | **0.147** | **57%** |

\*Top-1 counts any move tied with Stockfish's best.

The jump from the first Laya fine-tune to **Laya v2** came entirely from the training setup (more varied data, rule facts in the input, and a relative target), not from a bigger model.

### Caveats

* **10 games is a small sample.** A score's uncertainty is roughly ±15–20 percentage points, so the 35–60% results among the top three models are not a firm ranking.
* **Matches are a blunt instrument here.** Between deterministic players of similar strength, most games end in draws, which hides real differences. The per-move evaluation above is the more sensitive measure.
* **Jev received rule facts and the "aim to win" instruction** in the tournament, like the fine-tuned models.

## Repository layout

Everything lives in [`laya-test/`](laya-test/). Run commands from that folder.

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `chess_game.py` | Play against a model, or watch two models, in a pygame window |
| `replay_game.py` | Replay saved games (PGN) with each model's reasoning; record MP4 videos |
| `laya_player.py` | Laya player; also the shared input format and rule facts |
| `jev_player.py` | Jev player (TypeSafe API) |
| `clm_player.py` | CLM player (talks to a `clm-serve` server) |
| `clm_chess.py` | Converts the data for CLM fine-tuning; evaluates CLM or Laya on held-out positions |
| `laya_arena.py` | Headless matches with full logging (moves, results, annotated PGN) |
| `tournament.py` | Runs the 9-match round-robin above; results table; exports games |
| `make_chess_dataset.py` | Generates Stockfish-labelled training positions |
| `train_laya_chess.py` | Fine-tunes Laya (single GPU, multi-GPU, or a quick smoke test) |
| `eval_typed_decisions.py` | Checks a Laya checkpoint on the public typed-decisions benchmark |
| `laya-chess-datagen.ipynb`, `laya-chess-datagen-v2.ipynb` | Kaggle notebooks: generating the two datasets on CPU |
| `laya-chess-train.ipynb` | Kaggle notebook: first Laya fine-tunes (2× T4) |
| `Chess_CLM_finetune_tournament.ipynb` | Colab notebook (A100): CLM fine-tuning, Laya v2, evaluation, and the tournament |
| `picked_games_*.pgn` | The games shown in the videos |
| `*.mp4` | Recorded replays |

Not included (too large for git): the fine-tuned model weights, the datasets (`*.jsonl`), and run logs. Regenerate them with the steps below.

## Quick start

Python 3.10+ (tested on 3.12, macOS on Apple Silicon).

```bash
git clone https://github.com/GauravAtavale/Chess_JEV_vs_Laya.git
cd Chess_JEV_vs_Laya
python3.12 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
pip install -r requirements.txt
cd laya-test
```

**Replay the games from the videos** (no models needed):

```bash
python replay_game.py picked_games_jev_vs_clm_ft.pgn
```

Games play automatically. Space pauses; ← / → step; ↑ / ↓ switch games; `--theme parchment` for a light theme.

**Record a replay as a video:**

```bash
python replay_game.py picked_games_jev_vs_clm_ft.pgn --one-game --seconds 0.3 --record jev_vs_clm_ft.mp4
```

**Play against the original Laya** (downloads the model the first time):

```bash
python chess_game.py --black laya
```

**Use Jev:** create a `.env` file in `laya-test/` containing `TYPESAFE_API_KEY=your-key`, then:

```bash
python chess_game.py --black jev
```

**Watch a match or run one headless:**

```bash
python chess_game.py --white laya --black jev --rules
python laya_arena.py --games 10 --player laya --opponent jev --rules --max-requests 60000
```

Each arena run is saved to `arena_runs/<date-time>_<matchup>/` (console log, every move with the model's scores, results, annotated PGN, and a summary).

## Reproducing the experiment

1. **Generate data** (CPU; Kaggle or locally with Stockfish installed). Two datasets were used: 20,000 positions from the first generator, and 10,000 from the updated one, which adds full game histories, deliberate repetition situations, games played out from won positions, and centipawn scores.

   ```bash
   python make_chess_dataset.py --positions 20000 --sample-rate 0.1 --out chess_20k.jsonl
   python make_chess_dataset.py --positions 10000 --sample-rate 0.1 --seed 11 --out chess_extra_10k.jsonl
   ```

2. **Fine-tune Laya** (GPU). The smoke test checks the pipeline in a few minutes, on any machine.

   ```bash
   python train_laya_chess.py --data chess_20k.jsonl chess_extra_10k.jsonl --out laya_smoke --smoke
   python train_laya_chess.py --data chess_20k.jsonl chess_extra_10k.jsonl --rules --target best --base laya_chess_20k --out laya_chess_v2 --epochs 2 --micro-batch 16 --grad-accum 2
   ```

   `laya_chess_20k` is the first fine-tune (trained on `chess_20k.jsonl` with `--target win`); the base model `convaiinnovations/laya` also works as a starting point.

3. **Fine-tune CLM** (GPU with ≥24 GB; a Colab A100 was used). Follow `Chess_CLM_finetune_tournament.ipynb`: convert the data with `clm_chess.py convert`, serve Qwen3-8B with vLLM, train the heads with CLM's `train/finetune.py` (`--task choice --loss softce --targets soft`), then serve them with `clm-serve`.

4. **Evaluate on the same 300 positions:**

   ```bash
   python clm_chess.py evaluate --data chess_20k.jsonl chess_extra_10k.jsonl --model chess --positions 300
   python clm_chess.py evaluate --data chess_20k.jsonl chess_extra_10k.jsonl --laya-path laya_chess_v2 --positions 300
   ```

5. **Run the tournament** (in the notebook, with the CLM server running and the Jev key loaded):

   ```python
   import tournament as T
   T.run_all()
   T.show_table()
   T.export_games(T.list_games(termination="checkmate"))
   ```

**Compute used:** Kaggle's free CPUs for data generation; Kaggle 2× T4 for the first Laya fine-tunes; a Colab A100 (40 GB) for CLM (about 10 minutes of head training after ~15 minutes of embedding) and Laya v2 (about 1–2 hours).

## Notes and known issues

* **Colab:** installing vLLM upgrades PyTorch, which conflicts with Colab's preinstalled TorchAudio. Run `pip uninstall -y torchaudio` after installing, in every new session.
* **zsh:** don't paste `#` comments after commands; zsh passes them as arguments.
* **Jev cost:** our player sends one request per legal move (~30 per turn). With rule facts, a game cost roughly 2–5 cents in the tournament. The arena caps each run with `--max-requests`.
